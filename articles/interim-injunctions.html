Interim Injunctions in Commercial Disputes: When Urgent Relief Is Available

Legal Insight · TEO JENG YU & PARTNERS

Some commercial disputes cannot wait for a full trial. Where a counterparty is about to dissipate assets, breach a restrictive covenant, or take an irreversible step, the only way to preserve the position may be to apply for an interim injunction before the substantive dispute is even fully argued.

The Threshold

Malaysian courts generally apply a structured test when deciding whether to grant an interim injunction: whether there is a serious question to be tried, whether damages would be an adequate remedy if the injunction were refused, and, if not, where the balance of convenience lies between granting and refusing the relief.

An applicant is also generally required to give an undertaking as to damages — a promise to compensate the other side for losses caused by the injunction if it later turns out to have been wrongly granted. This is not a formality; an injunction obtained on a weak case can expose the applicant to a real damages claim.

Common Applications

A Mareva injunction (asset freezing order) restrains a party from dealing with or dissipating assets pending judgment, and is typically sought where there is a real risk that a defendant will move assets out of reach before a judgment can be enforced.

An Anton Piller order allows for the preservation of evidence, typically where there is a real risk that a party will destroy or conceal documents or material relevant to the dispute if given notice.

Prohibitory injunctions are used more broadly, including to restrain a threatened breach of contract, misuse of confidential information, or breach of a restrictive covenant such as a non-compete or non-solicitation clause.

Why Speed and Evidence Matter

Urgent applications are, by their nature, made on incomplete information and under time pressure. Courts expect an applicant to have moved promptly once the risk became apparent, and delay in applying can itself count against the applicant when the balance of convenience is assessed.

Because the application is often made without full disclosure from the other side (and sometimes without notice to them at all), the applicant has a duty of full and frank disclosure — failing to disclose facts that might have affected the court's decision can result in the injunction being set aside later, regardless of the underlying merits.

What This Means for Corporations

For corporations and financial institutions, the practical lesson is less about the legal test and more about internal readiness: identifying early that urgent relief may be needed, gathering supporting evidence quickly, and being prepared to move within days, not weeks, once the need becomes apparent.

  • Assess whether damages would genuinely compensate for the harm, or whether urgent relief is truly necessary
  • Gather clear, contemporaneous evidence to support the application before delay undermines it
  • Understand the undertaking as to damages and the exposure it creates if the application fails
  • Move quickly — delay is one of the most common reasons urgent applications are refused

Our practice advises corporations and financial institutions on urgent applications, including injunctions and related interim relief, within the context of a wider commercial dispute strategy.

Where urgent relief may be needed, being prepared to act within days — not after the position has already deteriorated — often determines whether it is available at all.
